About

I'm a Master's researcher in astrophysics at UniSQ, moving into a PhD from 2027. 02 — Research

How do supermassive black holes shape the galaxies around them?

Reading a galaxy's history in the light of its stars.

Almost every large galaxy has a supermassive black hole at its centre. When gas falls onto it, the black hole lights up as an active galactic nucleus (AGN), one of the brightest objects in the Universe. Theory says the energy from AGN should heat or blow away gas and shut down star formation in their host galaxies. Direct evidence of this in real galaxies, however, is still hard to find.

My master's research at the University of Southern Queensland looks for that evidence in the galaxies' stars. Using integral field spectroscopy from the Very Large Telescope's MUSE instrument, I mapped stellar ages and chemical compositions across 32 nearby galaxies with bright, unobscured AGN from the Close AGN Reference Survey (CARS). I compared them with 46 carefully matched galaxies whose black holes are quiet.

What I found

The AGN host galaxies show no sign that their black holes are shutting down star formation. Instead, their centres are younger relative to their outer regions than those of quiet galaxies. This points to a shared gas supply that fuels both the black hole and new stars. It suggests the AGN we see today have not yet been active long enough to leave a lasting mark. Each AGN shines brightly for only a brief part of its life, while stars record billions of years of history.

What's next

I'm supervised by Dr Rebecca McElroy, and I plan to continue this work as a PhD. That work will combine multiple telescopes and wavelengths to trace when, and whether, black holes switch galaxies off.
